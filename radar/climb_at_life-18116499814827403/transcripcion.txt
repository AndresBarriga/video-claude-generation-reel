[  0.0–  5.1] You're not too weak to hang on. You're squeezing too hard. You squeeze every hold,
[  5.1– 11.0] bend your arms, and pull. That's not climbing, that's a pull-up. 30 seconds in, your fingers open.
[ 11.5– 18.2] In 2017, Alex Honnold climbed 2,900 feet of El Capitan with no rope, and was still climbing
[ 18.2– 23.8] after three hours. He wasn't squeezing the wall, he was standing on it. So how do you hang on
[ 23.8– 29.2] without squeezing? A sports engineer named Franz Fuss wired a World Cup hold with force
[ 29.2– 33.4] sensors. The better the climber, the lighter they pressed, and the sooner they let go.
[ 33.4– 40.3] Let's ask the climber app. One, your fingers are hooks. Squeeze until you stop slipping, no more.
[ 40.3– 44.8] Two, your arms are chains. Keep them straight so your bones take the weight.
[ 44.8– 52.0] Three, your legs are springs. Hips to the wall, then push. Hook, chain, spring. Climber watches
[ 52.0– 57.6] your arms. Long arms, light hands. Hang bent too long, and it tells you. Now watch Honnold
[ 57.6– 63.6] on the slab. Hips in, arms long, and his hands barely touch the rock. Don't squeeze the wall.
